# 01. FRB Balance Sheet

## Why Study the Balance Sheet?

The Federal Reserve (FRB) is often described simply as the central bank that sets interest rates. However, the best way to understand what the Federal Reserve actually does is not to start with financial news, but with its **balance sheet**.

When analyzing a company, the first documents to examine are:

- Balance Sheet
- Income Statement
- Cash Flow Statement

The same principle applies to the Federal Reserve.

The difference is that the FRB is not a profit-seeking company. Instead, it is an institution responsible for managing the **credit and liquidity of the U.S. dollar**.

---

# How the FRB Differs from an Ordinary Company

A typical company's balance sheet looks like this:

```text
Assets

• Cash
• Accounts Receivable
• Land
• Equipment

----------------------------

Liabilities

• Loans
• Bonds

Equity
```

The Federal Reserve's balance sheet looks very different.

```text
Assets

• Treasuries
• Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS)
• Repos
• Loans

----------------------------

Liabilities

• Federal Reserve Notes
• Bank Reserves
• Reverse Repo
• Treasury General Account
```

The first question most people ask is:

**Why is U.S. currency recorded as a liability?**

Understanding this is the first step toward understanding central bank accounting.

---

# Why the U.S. Dollar Is a Liability

Suppose you own a $100 bill.

From your perspective, your balance sheet shows:

```text
Assets

$100
```

From the Federal Reserve's perspective, however, it appears as:

```text
Liabilities

$100
```

In other words,

**your asset is the Federal Reserve's liability.**

At first glance, this seems counterintuitive.

The reason is that every dollar represents **credit issued by the Federal Reserve**.

Historically, U.S. dollars were redeemable for gold. Although the gold standard no longer exists, issued currency is still recorded as a liability on the Federal Reserve's balance sheet.

---

# What Are the FRB's Assets?

In accounting,

**an increase in assets must be matched by an increase in liabilities or equity.**

The Federal Reserve follows the same principle.

For example, suppose the Federal Reserve purchases $100 worth of U.S. Treasuries from a commercial bank.

Its balance sheet changes as follows:

```text
Assets

+100 Treasuries

----------------------------

Liabilities

+100 Bank Reserves
```

The commercial bank receives additional reserve balances held at the Federal Reserve.

This is the basic accounting structure behind **Quantitative Easing (QE)**, which will be discussed later.

The important point is that the Federal Reserve does not simply "print money."

Instead,

**it acquires assets while simultaneously supplying new dollars to the financial system.**

---

# The Largest Assets Held by the FRB

The Federal Reserve's largest asset category is:

- U.S. Treasuries

The second largest is:

- Mortgage-Backed Securities (MBS)

This also makes the Federal Reserve one of the world's largest holders of fixed-income securities.

---

# Understanding the Liability Side

The liability side of the balance sheet is equally important.

```text
Liabilities

• Federal Reserve Notes
• Bank Reserves
• Reverse Repo
• Treasury General Account
```

For now, it is sufficient to understand them at a high level.

### Federal Reserve Notes

Physical U.S. currency circulating in the economy.

---

### Bank Reserves

Reserve deposits held by commercial banks at the Federal Reserve.

This is one of the most important indicators of liquidity in the banking system.

---

### Reverse Repo

A mechanism used to temporarily absorb liquidity from financial markets.

---

### Treasury General Account

The U.S. Treasury's account at the Federal Reserve.

It is closely related to government spending and Treasury issuance.

---

# Key Takeaways

For an ordinary company,

**the asset side tells us what the company owns.**

For the Federal Reserve,

the asset side tells us

**how dollars have been supplied to the financial system.**

Likewise,

the liability side tells us

**where those dollars currently exist within the financial system.**

The Federal Reserve's balance sheet is therefore much more than an accounting statement.

It serves as a map of the global dollar liquidity and credit system.

---

# Learning Note

The biggest realization from today's study is that describing the Federal Reserve simply as "the institution that sets interest rates" is an incomplete understanding.

To understand the FRB, it is more useful to examine its balance sheet than to follow monetary policy headlines.

The asset side records how dollars have been created and supplied to the financial system, while the liability side shows where those dollars currently reside.

One particularly important insight is that the U.S. dollars we hold as assets are recorded as liabilities on the Federal Reserve's own balance sheet.

Although this study began as an attempt to understand the FIMA Repo Facility, I realized that the institution itself must be understood before studying its policy tools.

Just as I have come to view AI infrastructure as an environment rather than simply hardware, I now see the U.S. dollar not merely as a currency, but as an environment of credit and liquidity maintained through the Federal Reserve's balance sheet.
