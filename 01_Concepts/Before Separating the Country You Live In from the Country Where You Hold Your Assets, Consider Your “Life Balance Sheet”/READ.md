# Before Separating the Country You Live In from the Country Where You Hold Your Assets, Consider Your “Life Balance Sheet”

In one video, the idea of “separating the country where you live from the country where you hold your assets” was introduced.

At first, I thought it made sense. However, after thinking about it for a while, I began to wonder why I still felt uncomfortable with the idea.

What I realized was that the video isolated jurisdictional diversification while leaving out the relationships among income, expenditure, and currency.

An individual’s life cannot function solely on the basis of where assets are stored, as might be possible for a nation or a large corporation.

In reality, the following three elements need to be considered together:

* Which currency you earn in
* Which currency you spend and live in
* Which currency and jurisdiction you hold your assets in

These three elements are not independent of one another. They remain continuously connected within a single life.

I live with my family in Singapore, and both my current life base and most of my future major expenditures are in Singapore.

Therefore, moving part of my assets into SGD, or Singapore dollars, is not merely a matter of overseas diversification. It also means aligning my assets with the currency in which I am likely to spend in the future. The question is which currency I will actually use.

The situation is different for someone who earns income in Japanese yen and expects to continue paying for housing, education, retirement, and other major expenses in yen, but moves money to Singapore and converts it into SGD for reasons such as asset protection, tax savings, or overseas investment.

That person takes on new burdens that would not arise from holding assets only in Japanese yen:

* Exchange-rate fluctuations between JPY and SGD
* Currency conversion costs
* International remittance costs
* Overseas account maintenance
* Tax and inheritance procedures
* The risk of converting the funds back into yen when needed

An attempt to protect or increase assets may instead create a new risk: a mismatch between the currency of daily life and the currency of the assets.

## The Problem of Moving into SGD After the Yen Has Weakened

If a large amount of yen is converted into SGD after the yen has already weakened, the amount of SGD received will naturally be lower than it would have been at earlier exchange rates.

Strictly speaking, the valuation depends on when the yen was originally acquired and on future exchange-rate movements, so an accounting “realized loss” does not necessarily occur at the moment of conversion.

However, in practical terms, the person experiences a decline in purchasing power:

> After the yen has weakened, I receive much less SGD than I could have received before.

Furthermore, if there is a possibility of returning to Japan and spending the money in yen, the person will later take on another exchange-rate risk when converting SGD back into JPY.

In other words, in exchange for jurisdictional diversification, the person may suffer a double blow by carrying two layers of currency mismatch.

## The Structure of “Earning in Japan and Living in Singapore”

This lifestyle model is not as simple as it may appear.

If someone earns 100 in Japanese yen and lives in Singapore, that person must convert yen into SGD every month or at regular intervals.

During periods when the yen is weak against the SGD, particularly now, the same yen income purchases less housing, food, education, and healthcare in Singapore.

This is not primarily an investment-portfolio issue.

It is a problem of income currency and living-expense currency not matching.

By contrast, if someone earns SGD in Singapore and also lives in SGD, the basic part of that person’s life is not exposed to foreign-exchange risk.

Only after that foundation is established does it make sense to diversify surplus assets into yen, US dollars, gold, or other assets.

A more rational order may therefore be:

1. Align the balance sheet of daily life with the currency of expenditure
2. Then diversify surplus assets across currencies and jurisdictions

## Jurisdictional Diversification and Currency Diversification Are Not the Same

These two concepts are often discussed as though they were the same.

For example, if someone holds US stocks through a Japanese securities company:

* The investment targets are US companies
* The effective valuation currency is the US dollar
* The account and customer-protection framework are under Japanese jurisdiction

By contrast, if Japanese yen is held in a Singapore bank account:

* The currency is Japanese yen
* The jurisdiction is Singapore

Therefore, moving assets to Singapore and converting those assets into SGD are not the same action.

More broadly, increasing the number of jurisdictions does not necessarily make assets safer.

Holding an overseas account introduces additional administrative costs and complications involving local institutions, taxation, inheritance, account freezes, and other procedures.

## What Is Missing Is the “Balance Sheet of an Entire Life”

The video discussed a static asset-allocation strategy:

> Assets are concentrated in Japan
> → Move part of them into overseas jurisdictions

In reality, however, the decision cannot be made by looking only at the balance sheet. Cash flow must also be considered.

The relevant questions include:

* In which currencies are current assets held?
* In which currencies are liabilities denominated?
* In which currency is monthly income earned?
* In which currency are monthly expenses paid?
* In which country will the money be used in the future, and for what purpose?
* When assets are inherited, sold, or recovered, which currency will ultimately be needed?

Only after examining all of these elements can the location of assets be properly designed.

For this reason, rather than simply saying,

> Separate the country where you live from the country where you hold your assets,

I believe it is more realistic to think in terms of:

**Designing income currency, expenditure currency, liability currency, asset currency, and jurisdiction as one integrated system.**

## Viewing an Entire Life as One Balance Sheet

When analyzing a company, looking only at its assets is meaningless.

Only when liabilities, capital, and cash flow are included does the true shape of the company become visible.

Is life not the same?

A life contains elements such as:

* Assets
* Liabilities
* Income currency
* Expenditure currency
* Jurisdiction
* Country of residence
* Nationality
* Where one expects to live in the future
* Which currency one expects to use in the future

These elements exist together as a single balance sheet.

Moving assets overseas does not automatically make them safe.

The meaning of an asset is determined only when the flow of income and expenditure, the foundation of daily life, and the asset’s future use are considered together.

For me, therefore, the important question is not simply:

> Where should I place my assets?

It is:

**How should I intentionally design my income currency, expenditure currency, liability currency, asset currency, and jurisdiction?**
